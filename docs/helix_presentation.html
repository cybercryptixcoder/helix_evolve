Can a cheap model, given a memory,
climb like a researcher?

Helix evolves programs that search for mathematical constructions. Two loops share one exact evaluator: a fast, memoryless mutation loop and a deliberate research loop that remembers what it learned. On a well-known open problem, one run climbed for 55 generations.

The problem

The second autocorrelation inequality

A known open problem in analysis, worked on by mathematicians since 2010 and since 2025 by AI systems (AlphaEvolve and others). Its constant, C₂, is unknown.

What kind of problem

An optimisation over functions: continuous, non-convex, with a non-smooth max in the objective. It is not a yes/no puzzle like NP-hard problems. There is no "answer" to check against, only a score to push up.

What a solution is

A non-negative step function: a list of heights. Its score is R = ‖f∗f‖₂² / (‖f∗f‖₁·‖f∗f‖∞), at most 1. R is high when f∗f (f convolved with itself) has a flat top.

Why it is a good test

Every solution is a proof: its exact score is a certified lower bound on C₂. Nothing can be faked, scoring is cheap and exact, and there is a public record to measure against.

The idea

Search like a population, think like a researcher

Evolve programs, not answers

The system writes searches

Each candidate is a program, construct(budget, start), that searches for a construction in 30 s. It starts from its parent's construction (warm start), so progress compounds across generations.

Two loops, one evaluator

Breadth and depth, separately

A wide loop mutates freely with no memory, like evolution. A deep loop forms a hypothesis about why something worked, then changes one thing to test it. They meet at the evaluator and share one population: a figure-eight.

The continuity hypothesis

Memory across generations

Evolution systems usually show the model only the best programs so far. My bet: a researcher who remembers what was tried, what failed, and why, makes better next moves. Atlas is that memory.

The rules I set for it

  • No LLM in the evaluator: results are measured, never guessed
  • The model proposes, code disposes: every claim must cite evidence
  • Informative progress: the score comes back with a detailed description
  • The published records are never shown to any agent

The memory

Atlas: a map the researcher navigates

Adapted from my personal-agent project, Avy. Letting the model steer retrieval worked better than dumping recalled context on it. So Atlas gives the researcher a map, and the researcher chooses what to open.

Layer 1

The log

Every program, plan and result, immutable. Nothing is rewritten or summarised away.

Layer 2

The tree of attempts

Who built on whom, and what each change did to the score.

Layer 3

The findings graph

After each generation, a separate agent (the Distiller) writes findings (works / fails / mechanism / question). Each must cite the programs that are its evidence, and findings are linked to each other (refines, contradicts, explains…). Code checks every citation and computes each finding's status from the measured results.

Two-step navigation

1. The map: the strongest programs and their ancestry, an index of the findings, and the last generation in full (the thread of thought is never lost).
2. Open: the Navigator picks up to 6 items to read in full (code, diffs, results, the evidence behind a finding). Only then does the Researcher plan.

Why not just append a journal?

I ran it against two alternatives. Control is the standard prompt: best programs only. Journal appends everything with a rolling summary. Atlas produced the best run. With three seeds each, the conditions overlap, so the honest result is "memory helped", not "Atlas wins".

What the graph did

Elimination. On its first plateau, the run listed every failed attempt and saw that they all smoothed the objective. It tried the opposite, turning the max into a constraint, and went from 0.879 to 0.928 in three generations. The graph is what made that move possible.

Where it struggled

Retrieval favours recent findings. In the long plateau (generations 29–38), it reopened the same few findings each generation, and the break came from the wide loop. Only about a fifth of the findings were ever opened.

The system

What goes in, what comes out, what is different

Input

One problem file

  • a statement in plain text
  • an exact scorer that describes each result
  • a trivial starting program ("Code Zero")

Nothing in the loops knows which problem it is solving: a new problem means a new file.

Output

A program, a construction, a record

  • the best program, and the construction it found
  • every program and result, in a tree
  • a findings graph: what was learned, with evidence
Different from

AlphaEvolve-style systems

  • they show the model the best programs; Helix adds a research loop that plans with memory
  • breadth and depth are separate loops, not one prompt
  • a small model: 30 s per program, 2 CPU cores
  • no training: unlike ThetaEvolve / TTT-Discover, which train the model at test time

What the traces taught, and what comes next

Runs that reached for the textbook answer stayed there (4 of 9). Runs that broke through either changed the kind of move or branched. The next version (v5) makes the researcher explicit: a falsifiable theory, an agenda with stop rules, guaranteed slots for depth, branching and exploration, and continuity built from the steps that matter.
